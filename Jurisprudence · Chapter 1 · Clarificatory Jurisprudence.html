1. Evolution of law

Law was not invented in one day. It grew from habits, customs and religion into written rules enforced by a State. Tap each civilisation.

  • Dharma meant duty, right conduct and law together. A king, a student and a trader each had their own dharma.
  • Sources: Sruti (Vedas, “what is heard”), Smriti (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada, “what is remembered”), Sadachara (good custom).
  • Kautilya’s Arthashastra dealt with State, taxation, courts and punishment, a very practical law book.
  • Ashoka’s edicts carved on rocks and pillars announced rules based on Dhamma to the public.
  • The king was under dharma, so even the ruler was not above law.
In daily life: A village panchayat settling a land quarrel by custom is the same idea as dharma and sadachara at work.
  • Sophists said law is man-made and changes with place. Plato (The Republic, Laws) wanted law guided by justice and wisdom. Aristotle said “law is reason free from passion” and separated natural from conventional justice.
  • Stoics taught one universal law of reason for all humans, the seed of natural law and human rights.
In daily life: When people say “no one should be tortured, whatever the local rules say,” they are using the Greek idea that some law is higher than legislation.
  • Twelve Tables (about 451–450 BCE): first written Roman code, displayed so every citizen could read it.
  • Jus civile (for Roman citizens), jus gentium (common to all peoples) and jus naturale (natural reason).
  • Justinian’s Corpus Juris Civilis (529–534 CE) collected Roman law and became the base of modern civil law systems.
In daily life: Like a government portal publishing a Gazette notification online, the Twelve Tables made law public instead of secret.
Remember: India (dharma and custom), Greece (reason and justice), Rome (codification and legal technique).

In daily life: Downloading a pirated web series breaks law (Copyright Act, 1957) and is also morally doubtful. Lying to a friend about why you were late is a moral issue, not a legal one. Skipping a red signal is both.
Link: law is the tool, morals give it values, justice is the goal. A law that is too unjust loses respect and obedience.

3. Nature and functions of law

Nature

  • Austin (1832): law is the command of the sovereign backed by sanction.
  • Hart (1961): law is a union of primary rules (duties) and secondary rules (how rules are made, changed, applied), tied by the rule of recognition.
  • Natural law view: an unjust rule is not true law.

Functions

  • Order and peace: prevents “might is right.”
  • Settles disputes: through courts and tribunals.
  • Protects rights and liberty: life, property, speech.
  • Social change: Roscoe Pound’s “social engineering,” balancing competing interests.
  • Gives certainty: people can plan their lives.
In daily life: Your phone’s “I Agree” on terms and conditions is a contract the law will enforce. An e-challan sent after a traffic camera spots you shows law reaching you even without a policeman present. UPI refund rules and consumer forums settle online payment disputes.

4. Rule of Law and law as a social fact

Rule of Law means government by law, not by the whims of persons. Even the highest officer is bound by it.

Dicey’s three principles (1885)

  • Supremacy of law: no punishment without breach of law proved in court; no arbitrary power.
  • Equality before law: same law and courts for all.
  • Predominance of legal spirit: rights come from court decisions, not just written promises.

India

  • Art. 14 (equality) and Art. 21 (life and liberty) carry the Rule of Law.
  • Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978): procedure that takes away liberty must be fair, just and reasonable.
  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): Rule of Law is part of the basic structure.
  • ADM Jabalpur (1976) upheld suspension of habeas corpus in the Emergency; Puttaswamy (2017) said it was wrongly decided.
In daily life: When a police officer must show you a valid reason and follow arrest procedure, that is Rule of Law. A minister paying a challan like everyone else is equality before law.

Keywords in your syllabus

Authority
Power that people accept as rightful (Austin’s sovereign). Law needs an authority behind it.
Autonomy
Self-rule: a person or group governs itself by rules it accepts (Kant: moral self-legislation). Fundamental rights protect individual autonomy.
Anarchy
Absence of an accepted authority and rules. Law exists to avoid it, though some thinkers argue for minimal authority.
Obligation
The duty to obey. Austin: fear of sanction. Hart: internal acceptance that the rule is a standard for conduct.
Opinion and Dictum
Opinion is a legal view or judgment of a court. Dictum is a judge’s statement: ratio decidendi binds, obiter dictum (said in passing) only persuades.
Law as a social fact
Law is not floating in the air; it lives in society. Ehrlich called it living law, what people actually follow. Hart’s rule of recognition exists only because officials and citizens practise it. Example: wearing a helmet becomes “law in action” only when people and police actually follow it.

5. Nature and scope of jurisprudence

From Latin juris (law) + prudentia (knowledge/skill): the study of law’s basic ideas, not of any single statute.

Definitions: Austin: philosophy of positive law. Salmond: science of the first principles of the civil law. Holland: formal science of positive law. Bentham split it into expository (what law is) and censorial (what law ought to be).

Scope

  • Concept of law, its sources and purpose.
  • Legal concepts: rights, duties, person, property, liability.
  • Link with justice, morals, politics, sociology, economics, technology.
  • Schools of thought: natural, analytical, historical, sociological, realist.
In daily life: Should a deepfake video be punished? Who owns data from your smartwatch? Jurisprudence gives the thinking tools to answer such new questions when no statute exists yet.

6. From police state to welfare state

  • The Indian Constitution is a welfare charter: Preamble (social, economic justice), Directive Principles (Part IV, Arts. 36–51), Art. 39A (free legal aid), Art. 21A (education for ages 6–14).
  • Schemes and laws: MGNREGA, National Food Security Act, Right to Education Act, Ayushman Bharat.
In daily life: A police-state view says the State only guards the ration shop from theft. A welfare-state view says the State must make sure the ration reaches you at the right price.

Short questions (answer in 3–5 lines)

Q1. Define jurisprudence.
Jurisprudence (Latin juris + prudentia) is the study of the nature, concepts, sources and purposes of law. Salmond calls it the science of the first principles of the civil law; Austin calls it the philosophy of positive law.
Q2. How does law differ from morality?
Law is made or recognised by the State and enforced by courts and penalties; morality rests on conscience and social opinion. Law covers outward acts; morals also cover motives. Law changes by legislation; morals change slowly. Example: theft is both illegal and immoral; a white lie is only a moral matter.
Q3. What is Rule of Law?
It means government according to law and not personal will. Dicey’s three ideas are supremacy of law, equality before law and predominance of legal spirit. In India, Arts. 14 and 21 and the basic structure doctrine protect it.
Q4. What is a police state?
A State whose chief duty is to keep order, defend territory and collect revenue, with little responsibility for people’s welfare. The ruler’s security and public peace come first. A welfare state adds education, health, food and employment.
Q5. What is meant by law as a social fact?
Law exists because people accept and practise it. Ehrlich’s “living law” and Hart’s rule of recognition show that a rule is law only when society and officials actually follow it, not merely because it is printed in a book.

Long questions (answer in 1–2 pages)

Q1. Trace the evolution of law in ancient India, Greece and Rome.
India: Law grew from dharma, Sruti, Smriti (Manu, Yajnavalkya, Narada) and custom. Kautilya’s Arthashastra and Ashoka’s edicts show State law, and the king was bound by dharma. Greece: Sophists, Plato and Aristotle debated whether law is man-made or based on justice and reason; Stoics proposed universal reason, the root of natural law. Rome: The Twelve Tables (c. 450 BCE) made law public. Jus civile, jus gentium and jus naturale developed, and Justinian’s Corpus Juris Civilis (6th century CE) shaped civil law worldwide. Conclusion: India gave duty and custom, Greece gave reason and justice, Rome gave codification.
Q2. Discuss the relationship between law, morals, ethics and justice.
Law is State-made and enforced by sanction. Morals are society’s sense of right and wrong; ethics are reasoned principles of conduct, often professional. Justice is the ideal of fairness that law seeks. They overlap (murder is illegal and immoral) yet differ (a lie is not punishable; parking violation is not immoral). Positivists such as Austin separate law from morals; natural law thinkers say unjust law lacks authority. In practice, laws that ignore morality or justice lose public respect, so a good legal system keeps all four in dialogue.
Q3. Explain the Rule of Law with reference to Dicey and the Indian Constitution.
Dicey gave three principles: supremacy of law over arbitrary power, equality before law, and predominance of legal spirit (individual rights shaped by courts). Criticism: modern administration needs discretion and special tribunals. India accepts the substance: Art. 14 bans arbitrariness; Art. 21 needs fair procedure (Maneka Gandhi, 1978); the Supreme Court in Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) treated Rule of Law as part of the basic structure. ADM Jabalpur (1976) is the low point, later disapproved in Puttaswamy (2017).
Q4. Discuss the nature and scope of jurisprudence.
Jurisprudence studies law at the level of ideas. Austin saw it as philosophy of positive law, Salmond as science of first principles of civil law, and Bentham split it into expository and censorial. Its scope covers the concept and sources of law, legal concepts like rights, duties, person, property and liability, the link between law and justice, morals and society, and schools of thought. It is also interdisciplinary: sociology, economics, medicine and technology raise new legal questions (privacy, AI, cybercrime) that jurisprudence helps answer when statutes are silent.
Q5. Trace the shift from police state to welfare state and the Indian position.
The police state limited government to security, order and revenue. Industrialisation, poverty and rights movements pushed the State into education, health, labour and social security, creating the welfare state, where law is used for redistribution and protection of the weak. India adopted this model in the Preamble (social, economic and political justice), Part IV Directive Principles (Arts. 38, 39, 41, 47), Art. 39A and Art. 21A. Laws like MGNREGA, the National Food Security Act and the Right to Education Act put it into practice. Critics point to implementation gaps; courts have often read welfare rights into Art. 21.
